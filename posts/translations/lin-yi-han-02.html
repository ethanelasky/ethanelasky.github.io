About Fang Si-Chi’s First Love Paradise, What I Want to Say to the Reader

A translation of Lin Yi-Han’s May 2017 talk on her novel
Author

Ethan Elasky

Published

September 6, 2026

A lot of people after reading will say this is a story about girls getting seduced or raped; summing this book up in one sentence is improper, but if you forced me to change that sentence, I would say this is a story about a girl growing to love her rapist. That sentence has the word “love” in it, and we can say, Si-Chi1 is fated to walk towards ruin without turning back precisely because in her heart she has tenderness, desires, love, and to the end in her heart she has sex. So this is certainly not a book of anger, nor a book intended to serve as an indictment. But today I don’t want to talk about “seduction” and “rape,” because anyone who reads this book, and doesn’t see seduction and rape, is playing deaf and dumb.

So today I came to talk about a larger topic. When you’re watching the news, when you see “victims” and “perpetrators,” those minutely detailed dialogues, the wallpaper patterns of small motels and apartments, the really lurid details, there’s no way you keep going, but today reading this novel you do keep going. Why? Because you found some aesthetic pleasure in the book — a feeling of pain and pleasure (the word literally fuses pain 痛 and pleasure 快). Let me misuse a Confucian saying, “knowing it cannot be done, yet doing it anyway” (知其不可為而為之),2 where you know you shouldn’t read, but you keep reading on. This aesthetic joy is what I came to talk about today.

Chekhov has a story called “The Man in a Case”.3 Outside of the main character’s raincoat is a case; surrounding his bag is a case; everything with its own case. Even cases have their own cases. My story is about a case within a case.4

Let me first talk about the inside frame. The inside frame exists as the character of Lee Guo-Hua. Lee Guo-Hua, by virtue of being a novel character, in real life has a model. This model is a teacher I know. Maybe people were able to tell this real-life teacher has an original form, and maybe people thought, the original form here is Hu Lancheng. So, Lee Guo-Hua is a reduced — and then reduced again — counterfeit of Hu Lancheng. The model for Lee Guo-Hua’s model is Hu Lancheng. What I want to ask is, all students of Chinese — including me, including Hu Lancheng, including Lee Guo-Hua, we all know that “a person’s word is faith”.5 Today I didn’t even talk about the ‘great man’ 大丈夫, ‘benevolence’ 仁, ‘righteousness’ 義, ‘writing as transmitting the Way’ 文以載道, ‘writing as illuminating the Way’ 文以明道, ‘starving his flesh and emptying his body’, ‘the flood-like qi’.6 None of these; what I want to talk about instead is small feelings and small loves; what I want to talk about is the tradition of the Shijing before its love poems were over-interpreted and misread by later scholars as political poems. We all know, “In the heart it is intent; uttered in words it is poetry”, “Poetry springs from feeling and is sensuously ornate”, and Confucius’ “The three hundred Odes can be summed up in one phrase: ‘thoughts without depravity.’”7 These students of Chinese, Hu Lancheng and Lee Guo-Hua, why, as we know, when one speaks poetry about feelings—when one speaks words about feelings—his words should come from the heart; this person has “will” 志 and “feeling” 情, “his thoughts should not be depraved” 思無邪. So the thing that pains me here is, someone who truly trusts Chinese, how can he betray this vast and surging linguistic context spanning over five thousand years? The question I want to ask is this one.

Lee Guo-Hua has lines, his “love talk”, where because the reader already sees him through a tinted lens and knows that he is a “criminal” they think that he is disgusting, but some of these lines, if you look at them alone, you might think they are beautiful (note that I’m using the word beautiful), he has some lines that are highly artistic, and has some lines where you can imagine if it were Mao-Mao speaking to Iwen that the lines would be moving. If you imagine Mao-Mao saying to Iwen, “This is all your fault, you’re too beautiful,” or if you imagine him saying to her, “Obviously I need an excuse; without one, what’s between us couldn’t survive, right?” or “You’re Cao’s robes wet with water, and I’m Wu’s sashes in the wind”,8 or “In love, I am an unrecognized talent”, these words are truly beautiful. What I want to say is, people like Hu Lancheng or Lee Guo-Hua, you can say that their systems of thinking are deformed; they raped, or sexually abused, others; and yet think to themselves, “all is harmony, and that too is good”.9 You can say that their systems of thinking are warped, but can you say that their systems of thinking are not exquisite, or if not that, not beautiful? Hu Lancheng said of himself that he is both laughable and detestable,10 because his systems of thought are so contradictory, so much so that there’s nothing that isn’t included, because he is so so narcissistic, and has infinite tolerance for himself. This worldview obviously has many cracks and what can be used to fix these cracks? Language, rhetoric, all types of metaphor, and so much so that this worldview in the end becomes indestructible.

I’ll read a paragraph from This Life, These Times [by Hu Lancheng] where he says,

I already have Eileen [Chang], but I also took up with Xiaozhou, and then with Xiumei. Whether this was right or wrong, I could only decline to look too closely. I could not even think further about it. In any case that is how it is. It cannot be explained, and that itself is how it is. ‘Among stars there are good stars, among rains there are good rains’; in the human world too, among principles there are good principles. Such a good principle is what Mencius called righteousness, and since it can also be flirted with, righteousness becomes benevolence.11

So you see, we know he raped Xiaozhou, failed Eileen, but in his mind he slips out of this frame; what we thought of as the thousand-times-tempered sincerity that a man of letters should have, in the end just returns to base desires.12

So what I want to ask here is not even whether art can or can’t be dishonest. Don’t ask Si-Chi if she does or doesn’t have love, because of course she does. I even think that Lee Guo-Hua in some points has love, but he doesn’t love Cookie or Hsiao-Chi (girls he previously raped), or Si-Chi, these little girls; what he loves is his performance, the context, the scene, the picture.

So with precisely the character of Lee Guo-Hua, I want to ask: can art have elements of ‘artful words and a pleasing face’ 巧言令色?13 I will always remember the time I found out [V.S.] Naipaul beat his wife.14 How my heart hurt. I’m a person with a superstitious faith in language and could not believe that someone who could produce an impeccable collection of fables could beat his wife. I later read Edward Said’s Orientalism, which refers to Naipaul as an Orientalist; later, I read Said’s autobiography and other people’s books. Other people mentioned Said, and said that he was a two-faced hypocrite.15 Just like peeling an onion back layer by later — you cannot trust anyone’s writing and character; it feels as though nothing in the world can be trusted.

We can reverse the question from before and ask as my second question: Is it true that art in nature contains “artful words and a pleasing face” 巧言令色? “Art” is constantly producing new forms, like playing with a cat’s cradle you have all sorts of transformations, all sorts of transmutations, but these techniques — are they nothing more than ‘artful words and a pleasing face” 巧言令色?

This is the inside frame. The outside frame is: as a novelist, this story tortured me, it ruined my life. But for many years, I practiced writing, grinding and polishing my pen; in the process of composition, I very consciously, lucidly wanted to reach a so-called artistic height.

My aesthetics include the idea that form and content are inseparable, or using André Gide’s words, expression and existence are inseparable (note that Gide said content is existence).16 In this story, the author often deliberately misuses allusions, or in diction often uses words’ divergent/secondary meanings; in the book there is a girl Fang Si-Chi, infatuated with literature and who is infatuated with literarature and yet still cannot fully digesting what she is reading17 — these two are one and the same.

I’m not saying anything grand here; I think that my prose is fallen, it isn’t anything like Baudelaire’s Les Fleurs du mal “she become very low, [low into the dust,] and out of the dust a flower bloomed.”18 It’s definitely not like that. We all know the phrase “To write poetry after Auschwitz is barbaric” [Theodor Adorno, 1949].19 My psychiatrist, after knowing me for several years, said: you are a person who has gone through the Vietnam War. Then, after a few more years, he said: you are a person who has gone through a concentration camp. Even later, he said: you are someone who has gone through an atomic bombing. Primo Levi once said, Concentration camps are the largest-scale massacres in human history.20 But I want to say, no, the worst massacre in humanity’s history is Si-Chi-style rape. When writing this book I looked down on myself. When people emerged from the concentration camps, survivors, when writing books, they often hoped that humankind would never again do that; but writing this book, I am certain, not even talking of the world, but Taiwan, that this will continue to happen, now, this moment, it is happening. When writing I hated myself a little, and had a feeling of humiliation. I think that my book is a book of humiliation. For this humiliation, I would like to import J. M. Coetzee’s “disgrace”;21 using the words of Si-Chi, Yi-Ting, and Iwen, this is unrefined writing, inelegant writing, and to misuse the Confucianism from above, this is writing that knows it cannot be done and does it anyway, because violence of this mass cannot be represented.

This story can actually be described in two to three sentences, graphically and explicitly. In two to three brutal sentences it can be said; it is, “There is a teacher who for years uses his authority to seduce, rape, and sexually abuse his female students.” Two or three simple sentences, and yet I used a fine brush, perhaps too fine a brush, to depict it. I don’t want to do reportage.22 I don’t have the intention or power to change society, and don’t want to be connected with those “big” words, and don’t want to connect with structure. Here, in this frame/taozi, I want to ask: as a writer — what is this perverted, writerly, artistic desire of mine? What, at the bottom of it all, is this desire that is called ‘art’? I often say to readers, when you feel pain as you read, that is real. But I want to say more: when you feel beauty as you read, that too is real, and it is constructed entirely out of words and rhetoric. This is the question I want to ask.

My conclusion is, I once was seriously obsessed with Eileen Chang, and no matter how much I hate Hu Lancheng, I still must admit, the chapter in This Life, These Times, “A Woman of the Republican Era,”23 is one of the most thorough essays about Eileen Chang of all time. My whole novel, from Lee Guo-Hua to the act of writing itself, is just a big act of sophistry, a big interrogation of the artistic concept of the true, the good, the beautiful. I want to use one sentence to close. Yi-Ting, recounting the whole story of the building,24 thinks, “it was not those who studied literature, but literature itself, that had failed them.”25
